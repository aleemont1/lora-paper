\begin{abstract}
  This paper presents \ac{LAMP}, a lightweight transport architecture that enables the continuous transfer of large data blocks over point-to-point Long-Range (LoRa\textsuperscript{\textregistered}) networks. To overcome the prohibitive Time-on-Air overhead and duty-cycle saturation typical of existing Stop-and-Wait solutions, the proposed architecture segments payloads into up to 255 chunks per transmission window. Each chunk is protected by a 9-byte Logical Header with compact 8-bit node addressing (supporting downstream IPv6 link-local prefix mapping) and a 2-byte \ac{CRC}-16 checksum suffix. The protocol provides four distinct operational modes combining connection state (Connectionless / Stateful) and reliability (Best-effort / {SACK}-{ARQ} feedback). By requiring receiver feedback only at sequence boundaries rather than per-packet, the \ac{SACK} mechanism significantly reduces acknowledgment overhead without incurring the constant over-the-air encoding overhead of pure \ac{FEC}.
\end{abstract}

\IEEEPARstart{L}{ow-power}, low-cost, and long-range wireless communication technologies
are becoming increasingly important in the context of the \ac{IoT}. Applications
such as environmental monitoring, precision agriculture, and autonomous \acp{UAV} require
reliable data transmission over extended distances under strict power and hardware
constraints~\cite{10807074}.

Several communication standards, including ZigBee, Sigfox, and \ac{LoRa}
have been adopted to address these needs, and \ac{LoRa}, in particular, has
emerged as the leading choice for low-power and long-range deployments thanks to
its robust modulation technique and open network architecture~\cite{croce2020demystified}.
However, conventional solutions based on \ac{LoRa} (particularly those following
the \ac{LoRaWAN}\textsuperscript{\textregistered} specifications) are optimized for small, non-frequent payloads~\cite{8016141}.

This limitation poses acute restrictions on data-intensive \ac{IoT} applications which demand the continuous transfer of large data blocks~\cite{multimedia-lora-survey, dare-lorawan}, such as \ac{FUOTA}, \ac{SLAM} map transfers in autonomous robotics, or bulk sensor log collection. Standard \ac{LoRaWAN} links require substantial transmission overhead, restrictive duty-cycle limitations~\cite{etsi300220}, and lack optimized mechanisms for handling large, multi-packet payloads in point-to-point configurations.

This paper presents the design and implementation of \ac{LAMP}, a lightweight transport protocol for point-to-point and local multi-node \ac{LoRa} networks. \ac{LAMP} supports payloads up to 62.2~kB on 255 byte \ac{FIFO} transceivers (such as the Semtech\textsuperscript{\textregistered} SX1262), operating independently of higher-layer middleware such as \ac{LoRaWAN}.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bitmap \ac{SACK} in a Compact Addressed Transport}: bitmap feedback already appears in SCHC ACK-on-Error~\cite{rfc9011} and MPLR~\cite{mplr-chen2019}. LAMP combines a single uncompressed bitmap, carried in one feedback frame and covering up to 255 chunks per round trip, with a 9-byte addressed header, four operational modes and a three-way handshake that negotiates receiver limits, directly over raw \ac{LoRa} and without rule contexts or gateway infrastructure.
  \item \textbf{Lightweight Packet Segmentation and Abstraction}: The protocol introduces a 9-byte logical header (with 8-bit local node addressing) and a custom \ac{CRC} integrity check on partial payloads, enabling out-of-order chunk reassembly on resource-constrained microcontrollers.
  \item \textbf{Modular C++17 Implementation and \ac{TDD} Validation}: The core state machine is completely decoupled from the physical radio driver through a \ac{HAL}. This design enables both native host-based unit testing (using the Unity framework) and physical deployment on Semtech SX126x transceivers.
\end{itemize}

\section{State of the Art and Design Space}
\label{sec:state_of_art}
Reliable packet fragmentation and transport over \acp{LPWAN} has been the subject of extensive investigation, driven by the expanding demand for data-intensive \ac{IoT} workloads~\cite{multimedia-lora-survey, dare-lorawan}. Existing approaches broadly fall into three categories: standardized LPWAN frameworks, conventional Stop-and-Wait ARQ, and specialized windowed batch transports.

\subsection{Standardized LPWAN Frameworks: SCHC and LoRaWAN TS004}
At the standardization level, the IETF defined \ac{SCHC}~\cite{rfc8724, rfc9011}, introducing a generic architecture for header compression and fragmentation over LPWANs. RFC~9011 defines SCHC profiles over LoRaWAN supporting No-ACK, ACK-Always, and ACK-on-Error modes. While SCHC achieves high compression for predictable IPv6/UDP flows (down to 1--2 bytes), it relies on pre-provisioned static rule contexts and gateway-centric infrastructure, making it ill-suited for autonomous, ad-hoc raw-\ac{LoRa} peer-to-peer or multi-node links. Similarly, the \ac{LoRa} Alliance standardized \ac{TS004}~\cite{ts004} to support \ac{FUOTA} via open-loop \ac{FEC} redundancy codes. While open-loop \ac{FEC} delivers multicast firmware images without requiring a reverse feedback channel, it introduces continuous over-the-air encoding overhead regardless of physical channel conditions and imposes substantial computational decoding demands on resource-constrained microcontrollers.

Bitmap-based selective feedback is not new in this space: SCHC's ACK-on-Error mode returns a compressed bitmap of the tiles received in a window~\cite{rfc9011}, and 6LoWPAN Selective Fragment Recovery confirms fragments through a 32-bit acknowledgment bitmap~\cite{rfc8931}. LAMP adopts the same principle but applies it end-to-end over raw \ac{LoRa} links, without rule contexts or IP infrastructure, with a single uncompressed bitmap covering up to 255 chunks.

\subsection{Point-to-Point Transports and Stop-and-Wait Limitations}
In infrastructure-free point-to-point and local mesh configurations, early implementations relied on S\&W \ac{ARQ}. However, empirical link evaluations by Cattani et al.~\cite{cattani2017} and network scalability analyses by Bor et al.~\cite{bor2016} established that per-fragment acknowledgments incur prohibitive Time-on-Air latencies and severe duty-cycle saturation when applied to multi-packet payloads. Under typical LPWAN channels, the round-trip overhead of waiting for an individual ACK after every single packet dramatically reduces effective throughput.

Open-source \ac{LoRa} libraries follow the same pattern. RadioHead offers an 8-bit TO/FROM/ID/FLAGS header with broadcast address 255 and a reliable datagram layer with per-message acknowledgments, timed retransmissions and duplicate detection, which blocks the sender until each message is acknowledged~\cite{radiohead}. The large-transfer service built into LoRaMesher~\cite{sole2022loramesher,sole2026largetransfer} applies stop-and-wait \ac{ARQ} with a \texttt{SYNC} handshake, per-chunk acknowledgments, receiver-side requests for lost packets and RTT-based timeouts, and was evaluated on ten real nodes in two topologies. LAMP differs in using one cumulative bitmap per repair round and in offering four operational modes. Section~\ref{sec:ns3_eval} compares LAMP with MPLR and with this service in simulation.

\subsection{Windowed Selective Transports: MPLR and Technical Gaps}
To mitigate Stop-and-Wait overhead, Chen et al.~\cite{mplr-chen2019} proposed \ac{MPLR}, introducing windowed batch transmissions acknowledged by a bit-vector acknowledgment packet (\texttt{BVACK}). While pioneering bitmap feedback over LoRa, MPLR was engineered as an application-specific image uplink pipeline with key architectural constraints: a 16-byte fixed header (allocating 4~bytes each to source and destination \acp{EUI}), rigid single-mode stateful batching (up to 40 packets), and an explicit 4-way teardown sequence (\texttt{FIN}/\texttt{ACK}) without run-time buffer negotiation.

\subsection{Architectural Contributions of LAMP}
\ac{LAMP} addresses these gaps by establishing an autonomous, lightweight transport layer operating directly over raw \ac{LoRa} PHY. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:arch_comparison}, \ac{LAMP} advances the state of the art through four core design principles: (i)~a compact 9-byte Logical Header (11~bytes over the air with CRC-16), reducing control overhead by 31.2\% compared to MPLR; (ii)~four configurable operational modes spanning connectionless and stateful best-effort and SACK-ARQ transfers; (iii)~dynamic buffer safety via run-time \ac{3WHS} parameter negotiation (\texttt{SynMetadata}) with automatic inactivity pruning; and (iv)~a pure C++17 core isolated from physical transceivers via an extensible \ac{HAL}.

\section{Proposed System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
\ac{LAMP} is a transport-layer protocol running directly over raw \ac{LoRa} transceivers (such as the Semtech SX1262), segmenting payloads up to 62.2~kB into chunks of up to $S_{\max} = 244$~bytes while minimizing Logical Header and \ac{OTA} overhead.

\subsection{Framing and Addressing Architecture}
Extending an earlier unaddressed prototype, the production architecture employs a compact 9-byte Logical Header (11~bytes over the air including a 2-byte Modbus \ac{CRC}-16 suffix, polynomial \texttt{0xA001}), shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:header_layout}. Adding 8-bit addressing (\texttt{srcAddr}, \texttt{dstAddr}) increases frame length by $<0.8\%$ at maximum frame size (255~B) while enabling session isolation, dynamic buffer negotiation, and direct mapping to IPv6 link-local addresses at edge gateways.

\label{sec:operational_modes}

Start-of-Message (SOM) and End-of-Message (EOM) boundaries are derived implicitly: $\mathrm{SOM} \iff \texttt{chunkIndex} == 0$ and $\mathrm{EOM} \iff \texttt{chunkIndex} == \texttt{totalChunks} - 1$. Carrying \texttt{totalChunks} in every chunk enables receivers to pre-allocate reassembly buffers and SACK bitmaps upon receiving any fragment, even out of order.

The 8-bit Node ID space spans \texttt{0x00} (unassigned), \texttt{0x01}--\texttt{0xFE} (254 unicast nodes), and \texttt{0xFF} (broadcast, restricted strictly to Mode~1 to avoid ACK implosion). Active and completed sessions are uniquely keyed by $(\mathtt{srcAddr}, \mathtt{messageId})$. The receiver retains the last $\texttt{MAX\_COMPLETED\_HISTORY} = 16$ completed message IDs for at least 120~s (and $1.2\times(R_{\mathrm{max}}+1)$ SACK timeouts, $\approx 100$~s at SF12/125~kHz) so that duplicate trailing chunks trigger a terminal SACK without duplicate application delivery; history for a sender is flushed when a valid \texttt{SYN} arrives, and the initial message ID is drawn randomly (never 0) to prevent collisions after reboot.

\subsection{Selective Acknowledgment and Retransmission}
\label{sec:sack}
In reliable modes (Modes~3 and~4), \ac{LAMP} implements a \ac{SACK} mechanism~\cite{rfc2018, lora-arq-sar} to recover lost fragments with minimal airtime. The sender streams chunks sequentially, asserting \texttt{FLAG\_ACK\_REQ} on the final chunk. The receiver returns a \ac{SACK} frame (\texttt{FLAG\_ACK}) carrying an uncompressed bitmask of received chunks ($\lceil N/8 \rceil$~bytes, LSB-first). The transmitter selectively retransmits only missing chunks with \texttt{FLAG\_ACK\_REQ} on the last repair frame, iterating until complete or the repair budget $R_{\mathrm{max}} = 5$ is exhausted.

If a \ac{SACK} is not received within the dynamic timeout window, the sender re-transmits the chunk carrying \texttt{FLAG\_ACK\_REQ} from the preceding round, repairing lost query chunks while prompting feedback. Timeouts and repair rounds share the common budget of $R_{\mathrm{max}} = 5$. The timeout is computed adaptively:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{timeout}} = \max\!\left(T_{\min},\;\alpha\cdot\left(\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{oA, DATA}} + \widehat{T}_{\mathrm{oA, SACK}}\right) + \delta\right)
\label{eq:timeout}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\min} = 1000$~ms, $\alpha = 1.5$, $\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{oA, DATA}}$ and $\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{oA, SACK}}$ are Times-on-Air queried from the radio driver~\cite{semtech-an1200}, and $\delta = 50$~ms accounts for transceiver half-duplex turnaround ($\delta_{\mathrm{sw}} = 25$~ms) and RTOS scheduling jitter (25~ms), yielding $T_{\mathrm{timeout}} = 1000$~ms at SF7 and 3927~ms at SF10 for $N=20$ (Supplementary Table~S-I). Under independent frame loss probability $p$ and error-free control feedback, the burst delivery success probability across $R_{\mathrm{max}}$ retries is bounded by $P_{\mathrm{success}} \le \left(1 - p^{R_{\mathrm{max}} + 1}\right)^N$ ($P_{\mathrm{fail}} = 1 - P_{\mathrm{success}}$), yielding $P_{\mathrm{success}} \le 99.87\%$ for $N=20$, $p=0.20$, and $R_{\mathrm{max}}=5$ (99.0\% in Monte Carlo simulations).

\subsection{Memory Bounding and RAM Footprint Analysis}
To ensure memory safety on embedded targets, receiver memory for an incomplete session is bounded by $M_{\mathrm{session}} = N\,(S_{\mathrm{pkt}} + S_{\mathrm{node}})$, where $S_{\mathrm{pkt}} = 255$~B (chunk buffer) and $S_{\mathrm{node}} \approx 32$~B (container overhead); metadata ($\approx 48$~B) and bitmask ($\lceil N/8 \rceil$~B) are below 1\%. Reconstruction allocates the $N\,S_{\max}$ output buffer ($S_{\max} = 244$~B), peaking at $N\,(S_{\mathrm{pkt}} + S_{\mathrm{node}} + S_{\max})$. For $N=20$ (4.88~kB), $M_{\mathrm{session}} = 5.74$~kB during transfer and 10.6~kB at reconstruction. With $\texttt{MAX\_CONCURRENT\_SESSIONS} = 10$, ten active sessions plus one reconstruction hold $\approx 62$~kB ($\approx 12\%$ of ESP32-S3 SRAM). Admission control rejects incoming sessions if slots saturate or free heap drops below a 16~kB margin.

\subsection{Session Control and Handshake Protocol}
To support stateful stream negotiation and protect resource-constrained receivers from buffer overflows, \ac{LAMP} introduces a \ac{3WHS} mechanism. Fig.~\ref{fig:state_machines} summarizes the session and data-transfer state machines, while Fig.~\ref{fig:3whs_sequence} illustrates the corresponding sequence flows.

The connection lifecycle follows a three-step exchange (Fig.~\ref{fig:3whs_sequence}(a)):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{SYN}: The initiator sends a \texttt{SYN} frame (\texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_REQ}) addressed to \texttt{dstAddr}, carrying a 4-byte \texttt{SynMetadata} payload (\texttt{requestedPayloadSize}, \texttt{windowSize}, \texttt{timeoutMs}, default 15~s; local wait 3~s). The responder bounds \texttt{timeoutMs} to 3--30~s.
  \item \textbf{SYN-ACK}: If resources permit, the responder returns a \texttt{SYN-ACK} frame (\texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_REQ | FLAG\_CONN\_ACK}), clamping \texttt{acceptedPayloadSize} to transceiver capabilities ($\le 244$~B).
  \item \textbf{ACK}: The initiator parses the \texttt{SYN-ACK}, adopts the negotiated payload size, and transmits an empty \texttt{ACK} (\texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_ACK}), transitioning both peers to \texttt{ESTABLISHED}.
\end{enumerate}
Once established, data streaming proceeds as in Fig.~\ref{fig:data_transfer_sequence}. Five deterministic fault-handling rules ensure multi-node stability: (i)~\textit{Simultaneous Connection Resolution}: The node with the higher numerical address yields and treats the incoming \texttt{SYN} as an inbound request, while the lower address responds with \texttt{CONN\_NACK(BUSY)} (Fig.~\ref{fig:3whs_sequence}(b)); (ii)~\textit{Implicit Promotion}: If the final \texttt{ACK} is lost, the responder transitions to \texttt{ESTABLISHED} upon receiving the peer's first data frame; (iii)~\textit{Admission Limits}: Receivers reject \texttt{SYN} requests with \texttt{CONN\_NACK(BUSY)} when session tables saturate, free heap drops below 16~kB, or a stateful link is already active; (iv)~\textit{Address Filtering}: Frames from unexpected nodes are discarded; and (v)~\textit{Handshake Timeout}: Lost handshakes abort to \texttt{CLOSED}. Formal algorithmic pseudo-code for receiver reassembly and transmitter SACK-ARQ streaming is provided in Algorithms~S-1 and~S-2 of the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{C++17 Implementation and Hardware Testbed Setup}
\label{sec:implementation}
\label{sec:hw_validation_setup}
The \ac{LAMP} protocol is implemented as a production C++17 library decoupled via a Hardware Abstraction Layer (\texttt{IRadio}, \texttt{IPlatform}), interfacing an ESP32-S3 microcontroller to a Semtech SX1262 transceiver via SPI and interrupt GPIOs (\texttt{EspHal}). Operational limits ($R_{\mathrm{max}} = 5$, $\texttt{MAX\_CONCURRENT\_SESSIONS} = 10$, $\texttt{PHY\_BUFFER\_SIZE} = 256$~B) reside in a centralized configuration namespace (\texttt{LoRaMultiPacketConfig}). The state machine was verified across 72 host unit tests in PlatformIO. The hardware bench comprises two Heltec Wi-Fi \ac{LoRa}~32~V3 nodes (869.525~MHz, $BW = 125$~kHz, CR 4/5, +10~dBm) under the ETSI EN~300~220-2 10\% duty-cycle envelope (Band P). Extended driver bindings and FreeRTOS task mappings are documented in Section~S-II of the Supplementary Material.

\section{Performance Evaluation and Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:eval}
To evaluate the reliability, throughput, and communication limits of \ac{LAMP}, we adopt a three-part methodology: a large-scale stochastic simulation campaign executed in ns-3; a functional validation of the firmware on two microcontroller nodes with emulated frame loss, compared run-by-run against the simulator; and a planned outdoor field campaign, which is future work. The simulation results characterize the protocol under published channel models, the bench shows that the firmware behaves as the simulator does, and neither measures propagation on real links.

\subsection{Network Simulation Benchmarking (ns-3)}
\label{sec:ns3_eval}
To characterize protocol performance under controlled fading channels, we developed a C++ simulator integrated into the ns-3 framework (version 3.48, development snapshot of 2~June~2026) and the \texttt{lorawan} module (one commit after release v0.3.7, \texttt{67bcaef}). The simulator reproduces the LAMPv2 frame (9-byte Logical Header carried as a real packet header, plus the 2-byte \ac{CRC}-16), all four operational modes, the \ac{3WHS} handshake and selective bit-vector \ac{SACK} retransmissions: the receiver returns the bitmap of the chunks it actually holds and the transmitter re-sends only the missing ones. Timers and the repair budget $R_{\mathrm{max}}=5$ mirror \texttt{ProtocolConfig.hpp}. As a baseline it implements Stop-and-Wait \ac{ARQ} (one acknowledgment per frame, up to $R_{\mathrm{max}}$ retries per chunk) with two timeouts: the rule of Eq.~(\ref{eq:timeout}) (``base'') and a tight timeout of one acknowledgment airtime plus 50~ms (``tight''). Two related protocols run on the same PHY, channel and seeds. MPLR~\cite{mplr-chen2019} is modelled with its 16-byte header (239~B of data per frame, no additional \ac{CRC}), batches of up to 40 frames acknowledged by one bit-vector packet, a \texttt{SYN}/\texttt{SYN-ACK} handshake and a \texttt{FIN}/\texttt{ACK}/\texttt{FIN}/\texttt{ACK} teardown; its article specifies neither timers nor retry limits, so we reuse the SACK timeout rule of \ac{LAMP} and $R_{\mathrm{max}}=5$. The LoRaMesher large-transfer service~\cite{sole2026largetransfer} is modelled as stop-and-wait with an 11-byte header (211~B of data), a \texttt{SYNC}/\texttt{ACK} handshake, one acknowledgment per chunk, up to 10 consecutive timeouts and a 20~s minimum timeout; we also run it with the tight timeout above and with 5 retries, the budget of \ac{LAMP}. Its receiver-side requests for lost packets are not modelled. All protocols carry the same 4.88~kB message, in 20 (\ac{LAMP}), 21 (MPLR) and 24 (LoRaMesher) data frames.

The channel combines a single-slope log-distance path loss, a log-normal shadowing offset drawn once per transfer and Nakagami-$m$ fast fading, with a transmit power of +24.2~dBm \ac{EIRP} (22~dBm PA, 3~dBi antenna, 0.8~dB connector loss) and a receiver noise floor of $-174\,\mathrm{dBm/Hz} + 10\log_{10}BW + 6$~dB. Table~\ref{tab:channel} lists the parameters, from the \ac{LoS} free-space case to the Urban-like stress channel. The Urban and Water scenarios are the fits of Petäjäjärvi et al.~\cite{petajajarvi2015} (868.67~MHz, car on the ground and boat on the water, SF12 data); other measured urban exponents range from 1.58~\cite{rademacher2021} to 2.71~\cite{dare-lorawan}. The Rural scenario is the re-fit of measurements by Chall et al. published in the review~\cite{azevedo2024}, not a value from the original article. The Nakagami shape factors ($m=3$, 1.5, 1) are modelling assumptions for which we found no LoRa measurement. Two stress channels reuse earlier, steeper parameters ($n=4.0$ and $n=4.5$) as explicit worst cases.

Reception in ns-3 is decided by comparing the received power with a per-SF sensitivity threshold. The thresholds of the simulator correspond to SNR values about 5~dB lower than the SX1262 datasheet figures ($-7.5$~dB at SF7, $-15$~dB at SF10). Absolute ranges are therefore optimistic by that margin, and the main results are reported against nominal SNR; calibrating the threshold on real links is left to the field campaign.

\subsubsection{Simulation Campaign Hierarchy}
The campaign comprises 295,800 independent runs (50 seeds per point, $N=20$ chunks of 244~B = 4.88~kB): Block~A (37.2k runs, static AWGN sweep $-25$ to $+5$~dB), Block~B (71.4k runs, channels of Table~\ref{tab:channel}), Block~C (2.8k runs, hardware bench model), Blocks~D and~E (108.6k runs, MPLR and LoRaMesher benchmarks on paired seeds), and secondary Blocks~F--H (75.8k runs, message scaling to 62.2~kB, ACK loss, and timer ablation, detailed in Supplementary Material).

$PDR_{\mathrm{app}}$ is the number of distinct chunks received divided by $N$. Goodput is the delivered application bits divided by transfer time $T$ (completion time for acknowledged modes, arrival of last chunk for best-effort). Sustained goodput under the 10\% duty cycle replaces $T$ by $\max(T, T_{\mathrm{air}}/0.10)$, where $T_{\mathrm{air}}$ is sender airtime. Crucially, when reliable modes (Modes~3 and~4) achieve 100\% $PDR_{\mathrm{app}}$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:ns3_urban}, this indicates successful SACK-ARQ recovery within the $R_{\mathrm{max}} = 5$ budget rather than zero physical loss on the channel.

\subsubsection{Results: static channel}
In the static AWGN channel (detailed with full curves in Section~S-IV and Fig.~S-8 of the companion Supplementary Material), above the reception threshold every mode delivers the full payload; the threshold lies at about $-12$~dB (SF7), $-17$~dB (SF9) and $-20$~dB (SF10), and the transition takes only 2--3~dB. In a static channel the benefit of selective repair is therefore confined to a narrow band (the 50\% point of Mode~3 is about 1~dB below that of Mode~1 at SF7); fading widens the transition and the benefit grows.

In the lossless region the SF7 goodput is 4.83~kbit/s for Mode~1, 4.78 for Mode~3, 4.70 for Mode~4 and 4.14~kbit/s for Stop-and-Wait, so the SACK modes are 14--16\% faster than Stop-and-Wait at SF7 (and 11--13\% at SF9 and SF10). Mode~4 is about 2\% slower than Mode~3 because of the handshake. Under the 10\% duty cycle the sustained goodput is airtime-bound at about 488, 156 and 85~bit/s for SF7, SF9 and SF10 respectively for every mode (484 and 84~bit/s for Mode~4 at SF7 and SF10): the choice of mode does not change the sustained rate, only the duration of a single transfer and its reliability under fading.

\subsubsection{Results: fading and shadowing}
Table~\ref{tab:range} reports the largest distance at which the mean $PDR_{\mathrm{app}}$ over 50 seeds (each with its own shadowing draw, identical across modes, so that modes are compared on paired seeds) stays at or above 90\%, and Fig.~\ref{fig:ns3_urban} shows the Urban curves. In urban propagation at SF7, Mode~3 reaches 4.6~km and Mode~4 4.1~km, against 3.4~km for Stop-and-Wait and 2.5~km without reliability (Mode~1). The gain of Mode~3 over Stop-and-Wait is 22--35\% for the Urban channel, 14\% for Water and 8--17\% for Rural. Stop-and-Wait needs an acknowledgment for every chunk, and every lost acknowledgment consumes a retry, whereas \ac{LAMP} asks for one SACK per burst. Mode~4 trails Mode~3 because the handshake must also succeed. With a 1~dB SNR grid, one grid step corresponds to 8--14\% in distance, so differences of that size (e.g. Rural at SF7 and SF9) should not be over-interpreted. The ordering is unchanged in the stress channels: Mode~4 reaches 9.6~km (SF7) against 9.0~km for Stop-and-Wait in the LoS-like channel, and 2.7 against 2.5~km in the Urban-like channel.

\subsubsection{Results: comparison with MPLR and LoRaMesher}
\label{sec:related_results}
Table~\ref{tab:related} compares \ac{LAMP} with the two related protocols at SF7; Fig.~\ref{fig:ns3_urban_related} shows the Urban channel, while Rural, Water, and Static AWGN channel curves are reported in Section~S-IV and Figs.~S-5--S-8 of the companion Supplementary Material. SF9 and SF10 yield identical relative performance ordering. In the static channel all acknowledged protocols reach 90\% at the same SNR ($-12$, $-17$ and $-20$~dB at SF7, SF9 and SF10): the differences appear after the threshold, and with fading.

In the lossless region the compact header and the single-bitmap exchange make \ac{LAMP} the fastest. At SF7 Mode~3 sustains 4.78~kbit/s against 4.48 for MPLR (+7\%) and 3.92 for LoRaMesher (+22\%), and delivers the message in 8.2~s against 8.7 and 10.0~s; Mode~4, which pays the handshake, is still 5\% above MPLR. Under the 10\% duty cycle the sustained rates are 488, 468 and 480~bit/s, a gap of 4\% and 2\%, because that rate is airtime-bound.

In faded channels, MPLR matches Mode~4 range across all nine channel/SF cases (Table~\ref{tab:range}), as $N=20$ fits one MPLR batch (handshake plus one bit-vector round). Mode~3 reaches 8--14\% farther by avoiding handshake overhead. LoRaMesher's reach depends on its retry budget: with 10 timeouts per chunk it matches Mode~4 (Urban/Rural) and Mode~3 (Water), but with LAMP's 5-retry budget it falls to Stop-and-Wait levels (3.4, 14.7, 9.0~km at SF7). Crucially, this extended reach incurs severe latency: at 4.1~km in Urban SF7, mean sender time is 191~s (20~s minimum timeout) versus 11~s for Mode~3. These baselines reflect reimplementations under identical PHY and channel models.

\subsubsection{Secondary Studies: Message Scaling and Loss Sensitivity}
Extended parametric evaluations are detailed in Section~S-IV of the companion Supplementary Material: (i)~\emph{Message Scaling (Block~F)}: evaluating transfers up to 62.2~kB ($N=255$ chunks) proves that LAMP's delivery range remains invariant to payload size (Mode~4 maintains 4.13~km in urban channels), whereas per-chunk retry protocols lose range as $N$ grows (Stop-and-Wait drops from 3.38 to 2.26~km); (ii)~\emph{Feedback Loss (Block~G)}: subjecting reverse SACK frames to 20--40\% loss reveals that LAMP preserves 76--90\% completion at 30\% forward data loss, whereas Stop-and-Wait plunges to 46\%; and (iii)~\emph{Ablation (Block~H)}: confirms that polling missing sequence boundaries avoids catastrophic retry exhaustion.

\section{Conclusion and Architectural Roadmap}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented \ac{LAMP}, a lightweight transport architecture for transmitting large segmented payloads over raw \ac{LoRa}. By combining cumulative bitmask SACK-ARQ with implicit frame boundary derivation, \ac{LAMP} raises goodput by 14--16\% at SF7, extends 90\% PDR delivery range by 22--35\% in urban environments, and eliminates per-packet acknowledgment airtime.

\noindent\textbf{Protocol Scope and Limitations}: Five architectural boundaries are identified: (i)~\textit{Capacity}: Single sessions are bounded to 255 chunks $\times$ 244~B $\approx 62.2$~kB (larger transfers require application chunking); (ii)~\textit{Memory}: Incomplete sessions hold $N\,(S_{\mathrm{pkt}}+S_{\mathrm{node}})$ bytes (5.74~kB for $N=20$) plus $N\,S_{\max}$ upon completion; (iii)~\textit{Security}: Frame integrity relies on CRC-16 without nonces; (iv)~\textit{Synchronous Path}: Transmit calls block while awaiting SACK feedback; and (v)~\textit{Evaluation}: Fading models are empirical published fits, and the bench emulates i.i.d. frame loss over a laboratory link.

\noindent\textbf{Architectural Roadmap and Future Work}: We outline a three-level architectural roadmap and future work: (i)~\textbf{Level 1 -- Addressed Transport (Current)}: 8-bit addressing, 9-byte header, four operational modes, and dynamic 3WHS; (ii)~\textbf{Level 2 -- Spectrum Access, Rates \& Security (Near-Term)}: CAD/CSMA-CA backoff under ETSI EN~300~220-1~\cite{etsi300220}, dynamic ADR via SACK feedback, TDMA channel access~\cite{wei2020tdm}, and AES-128-GCM; (iii)~\textbf{Level 3 -- Multi-Hop Mesh \& IPv6 Extension (Long-Term)}: Dynamic multi-hop routing (AODV-Light / RPL adaptation) with hop-by-hop vs. end-to-end SACK~\cite{kim2022multihop, wong2024mesh, sole2026largetransfer} and IPv6 link-local bridging (\texttt{fe80::/64}); and (iv)~\textbf{Future Work}: Outdoor field campaigns (sweeping 1 to 5000~m in LoS/NLoS) are planned to characterize shadow fading, calibrate hardware sensitivity offsets, and validate multi-node coexistence~\cite{cattani2017}.
