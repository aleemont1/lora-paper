\begin{abstract}
  This paper presents \ac{LAMP}, a lightweight transport architecture that enables the continuous transfer of large data blocks over point-to-point Long-Range (LoRa\textsuperscript{\textregistered}) networks. To overcome the severe Time-on-Air overhead and duty-cycle saturation typical of existing Stop-and-Wait solutions, the proposed architecture segments payloads into up to 255 chunks per transmission window. Each chunk is protected by a 9-byte Logical Header with IPv6-ready node addressing and a 2-byte \ac{CRC}-16 checksum suffix. The protocol provides four distinct operational modes combining connection state (Connectionless / Stateful) and reliability (Best-effort / {SACK}-{ARQ} feedback). By requiring receiver feedback only at sequence boundaries rather than per-packet, the \ac{SACK} mechanism significantly reduces acknowledgment overhead without incurring the constant over-the-air encoding overhead of pure \ac{FEC}.
\end{abstract}

\IEEEPARstart{L}{ow-power}, low-cost, and long-range wireless communication technologies
are becoming increasingly important in the context of the \ac{IoT}. Applications
such as environmental monitoring, precision agriculture, and autonomous \acp{UAV} require
reliable data transmission over extended distances under strict power and hardware
constraints~\cite{10807074}.

Several communication standards, including ZigBee, Sigfox, and \ac{LoRa}
have been adopted to address these needs, and \ac{LoRa}, in particular, has
emerged as the leading choice for low-power and long-range deployments thanks to
its robust modulation technique and open network architecture~\cite{8424846}.
However, conventional solutions based on \ac{LoRa} (particularly those following
the LoRaWAN\textsuperscript{\textregistered} specifications) are optimized for small, non-frequent payloads~\cite{8016141}.

This limitation poses severe restrictions on data-intensive \ac{IoT} applications which demand the continuous transfer of large data blocks~\cite{multimedia-lora-survey, dare-lorawan}, such as \ac{FUOTA}, \ac{SLAM} map transfers in autonomous robotics, or bulk sensor log collection. Standard \ac{LoRaWAN} links require substantial transmission overhead, restrictive duty-cycle limitations~\cite{etsi300220}, and lack optimized mechanisms for handling large, multi-packet payloads in point-to-point configurations.

This paper presents the design and implementation of \ac{LAMP}, a lightweight transport protocol for point-to-point and local multi-node \ac{LoRa} networks. LAMP supports payloads up to 62.2~KB on 255-byte-\ac{FIFO} transceivers (such as the Semtech\textsuperscript{\textregistered} SX1262), operating independently of higher-layer middleware such as \ac{LoRaWAN}.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bitmap-Based \ac{SACK} Scheme}: Unlike existing \ac{LPWAN} fragmentation solutions that rely on Stop-and-Wait (S\&W) \ac{ARQ}, which requires one acknowledgment per packet and incurs severe latency under duty-cycle constraints, the proposed protocol uses a compact binary bitmap carried in a single feedback frame. The receiver reports the delivery status of up to 255 chunks per round-trip, reducing retransmission overhead and over-the-air footprint.
  \item \textbf{Lightweight Packet Segmentation and Abstraction}: The protocol introduces a 9-byte logical header (with 8-bit IPv6-ready node addressing) and a custom \ac{CRC} integrity check on partial payloads, enabling out-of-order chunk reassembly on resource-constrained microcontrollers.
  \item \textbf{Modular C++17 Implementation and \ac{TDD} Validation}: The core state machine is completely decoupled from the physical radio driver through a \ac{HAL}. This design enables both native host-based unit testing (using the Unity framework) and physical deployment on Semtech SX126x transceivers.
\end{itemize}

\section{State of the Art}
\label{sec:state_of_art}
Reliable packet fragmentation and reassembly over \acp{LPWAN} has been the subject of active research, driven by the growing demand for data-intensive \ac{IoT} applications.

A prominent industrial standard is \ac{SCHC}~\cite{rfc8724}, which introduces a framework for fragmenting IPv6 packets over resource-constrained networks. To guarantee reliability, \ac{SCHC} ACK frames carry a bitmap representing the status of individual tiles within a window (ACK-on-Error). However, \ac{SCHC} is designed as a generic adaptation layer for IP-based architectures, requiring pre-shared context rules and gateway-centric networks. Similarly, the \ac{LoRa} Alliance standardized the Fragmented Data Block Transport specification to support \ac{FUOTA} using \ac{FEC} codes to reconstruct blocks without feedback. However, \ac{FEC}-based recovery introduces a constant over-the-air encoding overhead and high computational requirements for low-cost embedded systems.

For custom peer-to-peer \ac{LoRa} networks, early efforts focused on overcoming the severe airtime latencies and capacity constraints of traditional Stop-and-Wait (S\&W) \ac{ARQ} schemes. Empirical link evaluations by Cattani et al.~\cite{cattani2017} and network scalability analyses by Bor et al.~\cite{bor2016} demonstrated that high packet-loss rates and strict Time-on-Air limits make per-packet Stop-and-Wait acknowledgments unviable for bulk data transfers~\cite{multimedia-lora-survey}. Chen et al.~\cite{mplr-chen2019} proposed the \ac{MPLR} protocol, introducing the concept of replacing Stop-and-Wait \ac{ARQ} with a windowed batch transmission scheme acknowledged by a bit-vector acknowledgment packet (\texttt{BVACK}). Subsequent specialized studies explored multi-hop relay networks for visual \ac{IoT} coverage~\cite{kim2022multihop}, comprehensive mesh survey frameworks~\cite{wong2024mesh}, and Time Division Multiplexing (TDM) scheduled channels~\cite{wei2020tdm}. As a pure transport-layer protocol, LAMP is topology-agnostic and can operate transparently on top of multi-hop mesh routing protocols to extend reliable bulk delivery across multi-hop topologies.

Building on the bit-vector \ac{SACK} concept, LAMP extends and generalizes this approach with several key protocol-level optimizations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Header and Protocol Overhead Optimization}: \ac{MPLR} relies on a 16-byte header per fragment (carrying 4-byte Destination EUI, 4-byte Source EUI, Service Number, Sequence Number, Flags, Payload Size, Batch Size, and Checksum). In contrast, LAMP utilizes a 9-byte Logical Header (1-byte~\texttt{srcAddr}, 1-byte~\texttt{dstAddr}, 2-byte~\texttt{messageId}, 1-byte~\texttt{totalChunks}, 1-byte~\texttt{chunkIndex}, 1-byte~\texttt{payloadSize}, 1-byte~\texttt{flags}, and 1-byte~\texttt{protocolVersion}). Including the 2-byte \ac{CRC}-16 checksum suffix, LAMP requires a total over-the-air overhead of 11~bytes per frame compared to \ac{MPLR}'s 16~bytes, yielding a 5-byte reduction in total over-the-air overhead per fragment.
  \item \textbf{Target Scope}: \ac{MPLR} was engineered specifically as an application-level image transmission protocol for agricultural uplink nodes to a central gateway. LAMP is designed as a domain-agnostic, general-purpose transport layer capable of segmenting any arbitrary data payload up to 62.2~KB on standard \ac{LoRa} transceivers (ranging from high-frequency sensor telemetry arrays and robotic \ac{SLAM} keyframes to firmware binaries and multiagent state matrices).
  \item \textbf{Flexible Operational Modes}: \ac{MPLR} operates under a single stateful batch delivery model. In contrast, LAMP provides four operational modes spanning \textit{Unreliable Connectionless} (best-effort datagrams), \textit{Unreliable Connected} (session-tracked streaming), \textit{Reliable Connectionless} (stateless \ac{SACK}-\ac{ARQ}), and \textit{Reliable Connected} (stateful negotiated streams).
  \item \textbf{Dynamic Parameter Negotiation}: \ac{MPLR} utilizes a fixed batch size and requires a 4-way handshake tear-down (\texttt{FIN}/\texttt{ACK}). LAMP implements a lightweight \ac{3WHS} embedded with dynamic parameter negotiation (\texttt{SynMetadata} and \texttt{SynAckMetadata} negotiating chunk size, sliding window, and inactivity timeout) and replaces explicit tear-down overhead with automatic idle session pruning.
\end{itemize}

To clearly position the proposed protocol within the current technological landscape and define the precise design space in which LAMP operates, Table~\ref{tab:arch_comparison} provides an architectural comparison with other prominent fragmentation and transport solutions over \ac{LoRa}, including Stop-and-Wait \ac{ARQ}, \ac{SCHC}~\cite{rfc8724}, \ac{LoRaWAN} Fragmented Data Block Transport, and \ac{MPLR}~\cite{mplr-chen2019}. 

Rather than claiming overall superiority over existing industrial standards, LAMP is designed to occupy a specific, previously unaddressed architectural niche: providing a \textit{lightweight, autonomous, and highly configurable transport layer for raw \ac{LoRa} links}. Each protocol in Table~\ref{tab:arch_comparison} represents a distinct set of engineering trade-offs tailored to specific deployment constraints:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\ac{SCHC}~\cite{rfc8724}}: Represents the de facto standard for IP-connected star networks, achieving header compression down to 1--2 bytes for standard IPv6/UDP traffic. However, it relies on gateway-centric infrastructure and pre-shared static context rules, making it less suitable for standalone, ad-hoc, raw-\ac{LoRa} peer-to-peer or multi-node links without gateway context managers.
  \item \textbf{\ac{LoRaWAN} Fragmented Transport}: Optimized for unidirectional downlink firmware updates (\ac{FUOTA}) to thousands of nodes simultaneously using Forward Error Correction (\ac{FEC}) without requiring a feedback channel. However, pure \ac{FEC} introduces constant over-the-air encoding overhead regardless of channel quality and imposes significant computational and memory decoding overhead on low-cost microcontrollers.
  \item \textbf{\ac{MPLR}~\cite{mplr-chen2019}}: Engineered specifically for high-volume agricultural image uplink transfers (e.g., 9~KB to multi-megabyte images) using 40-packet batching with bit-vector acknowledgments over a 16-bit sequence space (up to 65,536 distinct message IDs). However, it utilizes a 16-byte fixed header (8-byte EUI addressing), operates under a single stateful batch mode, and requires explicit 4-way handshake teardown (\texttt{FIN}/\texttt{ACK}).
  \item \textbf{Stop-and-Wait \ac{ARQ}}: Offers minimal header size (2--4 bytes) and trivial stateless implementation for isolated single-packet telemetry. However, it incurs prohibitive airtime latencies and severe ETSI duty-cycle saturation when applied to multi-fragment payloads due to per-packet ACK waiting delays.
  \item \textbf{LAMP (Proposed Protocol)}: Fills the design space of standalone raw-\ac{LoRa} links by providing four configurable operational modes (Reliable/Unreliable $\times$ Connected/Connectionless), 8-bit local unicast/broadcast addressing, dynamic \ac{3WHS} buffer negotiation, and transport-layer purity (decoupled from opt-in HAL channel access helpers like CAD/CSMA-CA).
  \textit{Protocol Limitations \& Trade-offs}: To optimize header efficiency, LAMP allocates 1 byte for fragment indexing (\texttt{totalChunks} and \texttt{chunkIndex}), bounding a single \ac{SACK} bitmap session to 255 chunks (62.2~KB total payload size, requiring sequential sessions for larger files). Furthermore, carrying 8-bit node addresses and sequence metadata requires a 9-byte Logical Header (+2-byte \ac{CRC}-16 suffix, totaling 11~bytes overhead per frame), and session tracking requires microcontroller RAM allocation for \ac{SACK} bitmaps (dynamically sized up to 32~bytes for 255 chunks, $\lceil \mathrm{totalChunks}/8 \rceil$) and millisecond timer primitives, unlike stateless Stop-and-Wait \ac{ARQ}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
LAMP is a transport-layer protocol running directly over the \ac{LoRa} physical layer. It segments arbitrarily large payloads (within the described constraints) at the transmitter and reconstructs them at the receiver while minimizing Logical Header and \ac{OTA} overhead.
\subsection{Protocol Specification and Design Iterations}
We describe the design of LAMP by contrasting its initial stateless baseline (LAMPv1) with the current addressed architecture (LAMPv2).

\subsubsection{Stateless Header Specification}
The initial protocol version, LAMPv1, was designed for maximum header compression during periodic sensor telemetry transfers over point-to-point links. It utilized a compact 7-byte Logical Header containing only sequence tracking and payload bounds (\texttt{messageId} [2~B], \texttt{totalChunks} [1~B], \texttt{chunkIndex} [1~B], \texttt{payloadSize} [1~B], \texttt{flags} [1~B], and \texttt{protocolVersion} [1~B]). 

While LAMPv1 achieved high spectral efficiency for isolated datagram bursts, deployment on embedded hardware revealed three key architectural limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Speculative Buffer Allocation}: Receivers allocated reassembly memory speculatively upon receiving the first fragment of an unannounced stream. When receiving large payloads (e.g., image tiles or firmware blocks), memory-constrained microcontrollers risked heap exhaustion or buffer overflows.
  \item \textit{Session Interference}: Lacking explicit source and destination identifiers, multiple unaddressed transmitters sharing the same RF channel risked interleaving fragments with identical sequence numbers, leading to corrupted reassembly buffers.
  \item \textit{Static Transport Limits}: Fixed chunk sizes and static timeout thresholds prevented dynamic adaptation to link quality degradation or variable receiver memory limits.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Addressed Logical Header Specification}
To resolve the limitations of LAMPv1, LAMPv2 introduces an addressed, stateful transport architecture. LAMPv2 expands the Logical Header to 9~bytes by embedding explicit 8-bit Source (\texttt{srcAddr}) and Destination (\texttt{dstAddr}) Node Identifiers, while incorporating an integrated \ac{3WHS} mechanism.

Although adding 2~bytes to the header increases over-the-air frame length by less than 0.8\% relative to the maximum 255-byte frame (9-byte header + 244-byte payload + 2-byte \ac{CRC}-16), this trade-off provides three main architectural benefits:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Buffer Memory Safety}: The \ac{3WHS} exchange allows nodes to negotiate reassembly buffer limits (\texttt{SynMetadata}) prior to payload transmission, preventing allocation failures on memory-constrained receivers.
  \item \textit{Channel Session Isolation}: Explicit node addressing prevents session crosstalk and frame interleaving in multi-node wireless topologies.
  \item \textit{IPv6 Integration Readiness}: The 8-bit Node ID introduces a lightweight local addressing namespace that can be mapped directly to an IPv6 link-local prefix at an edge gateway, enabling transparent IP-layer integration without transmitting heavy 128-bit IPv6 headers over raw \ac{LoRa} radio links.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Operational Modes and Sequence Management}
To accommodate diverse \ac{IoT} workload requirements, LAMP defines four distinct operational modes across two primary dimensions: \textit{Reliability} (Unreliable Best-Effort vs. \ac{SACK}-\ac{ARQ}) and \textit{Connection State} (Connectionless Handshake-Free vs. Connection-Oriented Stateful).

\subsubsection{Implicit Frame Boundary Derivation}
To maintain a compact 9-byte header while supporting robust out-of-order packet reassembly, LAMP avoids dedicated boundary control bits. Instead, Start-of-Message (SOM) and End-of-Message (EOM) boundaries are implicitly derived at the receiver directly from the 0-based fragment index (\texttt{chunkIndex}) and the total fragment count (\texttt{totalChunks}):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SOM} \iff \texttt{chunkIndex} == 0
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EOM} \iff \texttt{chunkIndex} == \texttt{totalChunks} - 1
\end{equation}
Explicitly carrying \texttt{totalChunks} in every fragment header allows the receiver to determine the exact message bounds and pre-allocate the required reassembly buffer and \ac{SACK} bitmap upon receiving \textit{any} fragment, even if fragments arrive out of order or if the initial chunk is lost.

\subsubsection{Node Addressing Scheme}
To support multi-node communication without incurring the prohibitive airtime overhead of full 128-bit IPv6 headers on \ac{LoRa} PHY payloads, LAMP introduces a compact 8-bit Node Identifier space:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{0x00} (\texttt{ADDRESS\_UNASSIGNED})}: Reserved for legacy or unaddressed P2P datagrams (promiscuous mode).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{0x01}--\texttt{0xFE}}: 254 unique individual node addresses (Unicast).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{0xFF} (\texttt{ADDRESS\_BROADCAST})}: Broadcast address processed by all active receiving nodes on the RF channel.
\end{itemize}
This 8-bit Node ID provides a lightweight local namespace: at an edge gateway or application-layer bridge, a local 8-bit Node ID (e.g., \texttt{0x0A}) can be mapped to an IPv6 link-local subnet suffix (e.g., \texttt{fe80::0a}), enabling transparent IP-layer integration without transmitting heavy IPv6 headers over the constrained radio link.

The 8-bit address space supports up to 254 unicast nodes per RF cluster, which is sufficient for the dense but bounded deployments targeted by this work (e.g., agricultural sensor arrays, \ac{UAV} swarm sub-clusters). For applications requiring broader network reach, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} foresees integration of full IPv6 routing, where the 8-bit node identifier serves as a local cluster suffix rather than a global unique identifier. Note that the 254 addressable nodes operate independently of the receiver's concurrency limit ($\texttt{MAX\_CONCURRENT\_SESSIONS} = 10$), which governs the maximum number of simultaneously active reassembly sessions.

The fields in the Logical Header are defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{srcAddr} (1 byte)}: Source node identifier (\texttt{0x01}--\texttt{0xFE}, or \texttt{0x00} for unassigned).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{dstAddr} (1 byte)}: Destination node identifier (\texttt{0x01}--\texttt{0xFE}, or \texttt{0xFF} for broadcast).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{messageId} (2 bytes)}: Unique message sequence identifier. All fragments composing the same segmented message share this ID.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{totalChunks} (1 byte)}: The total count of chunks into which the original large payload is divided ($1 \le \texttt{totalChunks} \le 255$).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{chunkIndex} (1 byte)}: The 0-based index of this specific fragment ($0 \le \texttt{chunkIndex} < \texttt{totalChunks}$).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{payloadSize} (1 byte)}: Payload data byte count. Logically, the 1-byte field supports up to 255~bytes per chunk, enabling a theoretical protocol capacity of $255 \times 255 = 65,025$~bytes ($\approx 65.0$~KB) per session. On standard transceivers with a 255-byte hardware FIFO limit (e.g., SX1262), subtracting the 9-byte header and 2-byte CRC-16 yields $N = 244$~bytes per chunk, resulting in an empirical capacity of $255 \times 244 = 62,220$~bytes ($\approx 62.2$~KB).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{flags} (1 byte)}: A control bitfield used to manage transmission and connection states:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{FLAG\_ACK\_REQ} (\texttt{0x04}): Selective Acknowledgment requested from receiver.
    \item \texttt{FLAG\_ACK} (\texttt{0x08}): SACK feedback packet containing binary bitmap of received chunks.
    \item \texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_REQ} (\texttt{0x10}): Connection Request control frame.
    \item \texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_ACK} (\texttt{0x20}): Connection Acknowledged control frame.
    \item \texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_NACK} (\texttt{0x40}): Connection Refused control frame.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{protocolVersion} (1 byte)}: Identifies protocol version (currently 2) to ensure backward compatibility.
\end{itemize}

After the payload, a 16-bit CRC-16 (Modbus CRC-16, polynomial \texttt{0xA001}, initialized to \texttt{0xFFFF}) is appended. The checksum is computed over the concatenation of the 9-byte Logical Header and exactly \texttt{payloadSize} application-layer bytes; any trailing buffer bytes beyond \texttt{payloadSize} are excluded, so that partial final chunks are validated identically to full-size chunks and inter-implementation interoperability is preserved.

\subsection{Selective Acknowledgment and Retransmission}
In reliable mode, the protocol implements a \ac{SACK} mechanism~\cite{lora-arq-sar} to recover lost fragments with minimal additional airtime.

\subsubsection{Retransmission Protocol Interaction}
When a transmission is executed in reliable mode:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The transmitter segments the payload, serializes the chunks with \texttt{srcAddr} and \texttt{dstAddr}, and streams them sequentially. The final chunk index ($Index = \texttt{totalChunks} - 1$) is marked with the \texttt{FLAG\_ACK\_REQ} flag.
  \item Upon receipt of the chunk requesting acknowledgment, the receiver evaluates its reassembly session. It identifies which fragments are still missing by checking its internal bitmask of received chunks.
  \item The receiver responds by transmitting a \ac{SACK} packet (\texttt{FLAG\_ACK}) addressed back to the transmitter's \texttt{srcAddr}. The \ac{SACK}'s payload contains a binary bitmap where each bit represents the receipt status of a chunk (1 for received, 0 for missing).
  \item The transmitter parses the received \ac{SACK} bitmap. If some chunks are missing, it selectively retransmits only those lost chunks. The last retransmitted chunk has the \texttt{FLAG\_ACK\_REQ} flag set again to query the receiver.
  \item This selective retry loop continues until the full message is reconstructed at the receiver, or the transmitter reaches the maximum retry count.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Half-Duplex Hardware Switching Guard}
Because \ac{LoRa} transceivers are half-duplex, a hardware transition period is required for the transmitter to switch from transmit (TX) mode to receive (RX) mode after sending a packet. While physical SX1262 transceiver state transitions take $<100\,\mu\text{s}$, the $\delta = 25$~ms guard delay accounts for physical transceiver switching alongside software-level Real-Time Operating System (RTOS) task scheduling jitter, Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI) bus transaction queuing, and microcontroller wake-up latencies on embedded targets.

It is important to emphasize that all protocol timing parameters, guard delays (e.g., \texttt{SACK\_PREAMBLE\_GUARD\_DELAY\_MS}), and retry limits are fully decoupled into a centralized configuration namespace (\texttt{LoRaMultiPacketConfig}) and can be customized by integrators for any MCU/radio target. The default parameters presented in this study were specifically tuned around the Heltec Wi-Fi \ac{LoRa}~32~V3 (ESP32-S3) hardware platform.

\subsubsection{Timeout and Fallback Recovery}
If the transmitter does not receive a \ac{SACK} packet within the dynamic timeout window, it assumes the query or response was lost and retransmits the last \texttt{FLAG\_ACK\_REQ}-marked chunk. The transmitter permits up to $R_{\mathrm{max}} = 5$ consecutive timeout events before aborting the session.

The timeout is computed adaptively as:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{timeout}} = \max\!\left(T_{\min},\;\alpha\cdot\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{oA}} + \delta\right)
\label{eq:timeout}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\min} = 1000$~ms is the minimum guard, $\alpha = 1.5$ is a safety factor, $\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{oA}}$ is the Time-on-Air for the current packet length queried from the radio driver~\cite{semtech-an1200}, and $\delta = 25$~ms is the half-duplex switching and scheduling guard delay. Table~\ref{tab:timeouts} reports representative timeout values across spreading factors.

\subsubsection{Analytical Burst Reliability Bounding}
Under a frame loss probability $p$, the probability that a specific chunk or \ac{SACK} query fails after $R_{\mathrm{max}}$ retransmission retries is $p^{R_{\mathrm{max}} + 1}$. Consequently, for a data payload segmented into $N$ chunks, the overall burst delivery success probability $P_{\mathrm{success}}$ and overall burst failure probability $P_{\mathrm{fail}}$ under \ac{SACK}-\ac{ARQ} are analytically bounded by:
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{success}} = \left(1 - p^{R_{\mathrm{max}} + 1}\right)^N, \qquad P_{\mathrm{fail}} = 1 - P_{\mathrm{success}}
\label{eq:burst_fail}
\end{equation}
For a lossy wireless channel with a 20\% frame error rate ($p = 0.20$) and the default retry limit $R_{\mathrm{max}} = 5$ (configured via \texttt{LoRaMultiPacketConfig::MAX\_RETRIES}), the individual chunk failure probability drops to $p^6 = 0.000064$, yielding an overall burst delivery success probability $P_{\mathrm{success}} = (1 - 0.000064)^{20} \approx 99.87\%$ for a standard 20-chunk payload transfer.

\subsection{Memory Bounding and RAM Footprint Analysis}
To guarantee memory safety on constrained microcontrollers, the dynamic memory allocated for an active reassembly session at the receiver is strictly bounded by:
\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{session}} = S_{\mathrm{struct}} + N_{\mathrm{chunks}} \cdot S_{\mathrm{chunk}} + \left\lceil \frac{N_{\mathrm{chunks}}}{8} \right\rceil
\label{eq:ram_footprint}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\mathrm{struct}} \approx 48$~bytes represents the session tracking structure metadata, $S_{\mathrm{chunk}} \le 244$~bytes is the maximum payload chunk size, and the final term represents the binary \ac{SACK} bitmask.

For a representative 20-chunk payload (4.88~KB data size), a complete reassembly session requires only $\approx 4.93$~KB of RAM. Furthermore, the protocol bounds the maximum number of concurrent active sessions ($\texttt{MAX\_CONCURRENT\_SESSIONS} = 10$), placing an absolute upper bound of $\approx 49.3$~KB on total protocol RAM consumption, making it well-suited for low-cost \ac{IoT} microcontrollers with limited SRAM capacities.

\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Received Frame $F$
\Ensure Reassembled Payload $P$ or Drop Status
\State $(hdr, payload, crc) \gets \text{ParseFrame}(F)$
\If{$\text{ValidateCRC}(hdr, payload, crc) == \text{FAIL}$} \State \text{Drop}($F$); \Return \EndIf
\If{$hdr.dstAddr \neq myAddr \textbf{ and } hdr.dstAddr \neq \texttt{0xFF}$} \State \text{Drop}($F$); \Return \EndIf
\If{$hdr.totalChunks == 0 \textbf{ or } hdr.chunkIndex \ge hdr.totalChunks$} \State \text{Drop}($F$); \Return \EndIf
\State $session \gets \text{FindSession}(hdr.srcAddr, hdr.msgId)$
\If{$session == \text{NULL}$}
    \If{$\text{AvailMem}() < hdr.totalChunks \cdot hdr.payloadSize$} \Comment{Conservative upper-bound check}
        \State \text{SendNack}(hdr.srcAddr, \texttt{BUSY}); \Return 
    \EndIf
    \State $session \gets \text{AllocBuf}(hdr.totalChunks, hdr.payloadSize)$
\EndIf
\State $idx \gets hdr.chunkIndex$
\If{$\text{IsBitSet}(session.bitmap, idx)$}
    \State \text{Drop}($F$) \Comment{Duplicate chunk, ignore write}
\Else
    \State $\text{WriteChunk}(session.buffer, idx, payload)$
    \State $\text{SetBit}(session.bitmap, idx)$
    \State $session.count \gets session.count + 1$
\EndIf
\If{$hdr.flags \ \& \ \texttt{FLAG\_ACK\_REQ}$}
    \State $\text{SendSack}(hdr.srcAddr, hdr.msgId, session.bitmap)$
\EndIf
\If{$session.count == hdr.totalChunks$}
    \State $P \gets \text{ExtractPayload}(session.buffer)$
    \State $\text{DeliverToApp}(P)$; \text{FreeSession}($session$)
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}

\subsection{Session Control and Handshake Protocol}
To support stateful stream negotiation and protect resource-constrained receivers from buffer overflows, LAMPv2 introduces a \ac{3WHS} mechanism.

\subsubsection{Three-Way Handshake Procedure}
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:3whs_sequence}(a), the connection lifecycle follows a three-step exchange:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{SYN}: The initiator sends a \texttt{SYN} control frame (\texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_REQ}) addressed to the target node's \texttt{dstAddr}, containing a 4-byte \texttt{SynMetadata} payload (\texttt{requestedPayloadSize}, \texttt{windowSize}, \texttt{timeoutMs}).
  \item \textbf{SYN-ACK}: Upon receiving the \texttt{SYN}, the peer evaluates available memory and protocol version. If accepted, it responds with a \texttt{SYN-ACK} control frame (\texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_REQ | FLAG\_CONN\_ACK}) addressed back to the initiator. The receiver clamps \texttt{acceptedPayloadSize} to its maximum buffer capability.
  \item \textbf{ACK}: The initiator parses the \texttt{SYN-ACK}, adopts the negotiated payload size, and transmits a final \texttt{ACK} frame (\texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_ACK}). Both nodes transition their internal state machine to \texttt{ESTABLISHED}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Concurrency and Fault Handling}
To ensure protocol stability in multi-node and lossy environments, LAMP incorporates the following fault-handling mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Simultaneous Connection Resolution}: If two nodes transmit \texttt{SYN} frames to each other simultaneously, a deterministic tie-breaking rule resolves the conflict: the node with the lower numerical \texttt{NodeAddress} yields and transitions back to \texttt{CLOSED}, while the node with the higher \texttt{NodeAddress} proceeds with session setup.
  \item \textit{Receiver Busy Rejection}: If a receiver receives a \texttt{SYN} frame while its active reassembly session is already locked by another node, it responds with a \texttt{CONN\_NACK} frame (\texttt{FLAG\_CONN\_NACK}) specifying a \texttt{BUSY} status code, causing the requester to back off.
  \item \textit{Source Address Filtering}: Initiators in \texttt{waitForSynAck} and \texttt{waitForSack} validate incoming control frames against the expected \texttt{srcAddr}, ignoring packets from unrelated third-party nodes.
  \item \textit{Incomplete Handshake Timeout}: If a \texttt{SYN-ACK} or \texttt{ACK} of a \ac{3WHS} is lost in transit, the receiver in \texttt{SYN\_RCVD} or initiator in \texttt{SYN\_SENT} automatically aborts and resets to \texttt{CLOSED} after an inactivity timeout (\texttt{timeoutMs}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminary Proof of Concept}
\label{sec:poc}
An initial prototype using commercial Ebyte E220-series modules validated the core segmentation concept. Field trials confirmed feasibility but exposed three limitations: (1)~the modules exposed a proprietary network layer that prevented direct control of physical-layer parameters; (2)~a large, unoptimized application header inflated per-chunk airtime; and (3)~no acknowledgment or retransmission mechanism was available.

These findings motivated the redesign on Semtech SX126x-class transceivers via the RadioLib driver, validated on Heltec Wi-Fi \ac{LoRa}~32~V3 boards. The revised implementation described in the following sections, is fully hardware-agnostic through the \ac{HAL} interface and incorporates the 9-byte Logical Header, \ac{CRC}-16 integrity checks, and the \ac{SACK}-based retransmission mechanism.

\section{Hardware Level Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
To validate the proposed protocol, a complete implementation of the LAMP library was developed in C++17. The design is modular, separating the transport state machine from physical hardware dependencies.

\subsection{Hardware Abstraction Layer (\ac{HAL})}
To keep the protocol core hardware-agnostic, a clean \ac{HAL} interface (\texttt{RadioLibHal}) was implemented. On the physical nodes, the concrete class \texttt{EspHal} wraps the ESP32-S3 hardware resources:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\ac{SPI} Communication}: Manages registers configuration and \ac{FIFO} buffers transfer for the Semtech SX1262 transceiver.
  \item \textbf{\ac{GPIO} Interrupts}: Maps physical pins, including Chip Select (NSS), Reset (RST), Busy status indicator (BUSY), and the hardware interrupt pin (DIO1) used to trigger RX/TX completion interrupts.
  \item \textbf{Timing Primitives}: Implements millisecond counters (\texttt{millis()}) and microsecond/millisecond delays (\texttt{delay()}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Host-Based Unit Testing}
Following a \ac{TDD} methodology, the protocol core (e.g., packet parsing, reassembler sessions, and \ac{CRC} checksums) was validated in isolation on a desktop host (Linux/macOS). The tests compile and run natively using the \textbf{Unity} testing framework integrated into PlatformIO:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Integrity Tests}: Confirmed the Modbus \ac{CRC}-16 computation on headers and partial/padded payloads.
  \item \textbf{Reassembly State Machine}: Validated reassembly handling of out-of-order and duplicate chunks.
  \item \textbf{Stale Session Pruning}: Confirmed that incomplete reassembly buffers are correctly pruned after exceeding the timeout threshold.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Physical Benchmarking Setup}
Physical tests were conducted using two Heltec Wi-Fi \ac{LoRa}~32~V3 development boards. The transmitter node was connected to a console runner to sweep payload sizes, while the receiver node operated as a standalone listener. The transceivers were configured with the following parameters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Carrier Frequency}: 869.525~MHz
  \item \textbf{Spreading Factor (SF)}: 7
  \item \textbf{Bandwidth (BW)}: 500.0~kHz
  \item \textbf{Coding Rate (CR)}: 4/5
  \item \textbf{Output Power}: 10~dBm
  \item \textbf{Preamble Length}: 8 symbols
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Regulatory Compliance and Reproducibility}
It is important to clarify the relationship between the LAMP transport protocol and RF regulatory constraints. LAMP operates strictly at the \textit{transport layer} and is entirely agnostic to duty-cycle regulations, carrier frequency, and RF power limits; compliance with these constraints is the responsibility of the MAC/PHY layer or the integrating application.

For the experimental validation reported in this work, the 869.400--869.650~MHz sub-band was selected~\cite{etsi300220}. Under ETSI~EN~300~220-1, this sub-band permits a maximum duty cycle of 10\% and an output power of up to 500~mW (27~dBm), providing sufficient headroom for the multi-chunk burst transfers required by the LAMP reliable delivery modes without incurring the restrictive 1\% hourly airtime cap of the primary 868~MHz sub-band.

Additionally, in the reference implementation validated on Heltec~V3 hardware, the optional MAC-layer helpers provided by the LAMP library, namely SX1262-native Channel Activity Detection (CAD) with randomized exponential backoff (CSMA-CA) and duty-cycle pacing, were enabled to prevent collisions and further reduce channel occupancy. These features are opt-in and disabled by default; they are not part of the LAMP transport specification but are offered as convenience primitives for integrators targeting shared ISM bands. All experimental results are therefore fully reproducible in any deployment targeting the 869.4--869.65~MHz sub-band with the same radio configuration.

\section{Performance Evaluation and Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:eval}
To evaluate the reliability, throughput, and communication limits of LAMP, we adopt a two-stage evaluation methodology: a large-scale stochastic network simulation campaign executed in ns-3, complemented by functional hardware testbed validation on microcontroller nodes and a planned outdoor field benchmarking campaign.

\subsection{Network Simulation Benchmarking (ns-3)}
\label{sec:ns3_eval}
To characterize protocol performance under controlled fading channels across extensive distance ranges, we developed a full C++ simulator integrated into the ns-3 framework (\texttt{ns3-lorawan}). The simulator reproduces the exact LAMPv2 frame structure (9-byte Logical Header + 2-byte \ac{CRC}-16), all four operational modes, the \ac{3WHS} handshake, and selective bit-vector \ac{SACK} retransmissions over a Nakagami-$m$ fast-fading propagation loss model calibrated for the Semtech SX1262 transceiver (+24.2~dBm EIRP, 869.525~MHz).

\subsubsection{Simulation Campaign Hierarchy}
To achieve statistical convergence, an extensive Monte Carlo campaign comprising 117,050 total independent simulation runs was conducted. The experimental space spans 3 RF propagation environments (\ac{LoS}, Urban, Rural), 3 Spreading Factors (SF7, SF9, SF10), 4 operational modes (Mode 1 to Mode 4), 5 random seeds per parameter set, and 10 payload size steps (scaling from 1 to 100 chunks) across an adaptive distance grid extending dynamically up to 23.5~km with early-stopping thresholds once reception falls to 0\% across consecutive steps. Each curve point plotted in Figs.~\ref{fig:ns3_pdr} and~\ref{fig:ns3_goodput} represents the sample mean over 50 Monte Carlo trials (combining 5 random seeds across 10 payload size iterations per distance step), with shaded ribbons denoting 95\% confidence intervals.

\subsubsection{Analytical Reliability Bound under Stated Assumptions}
To model payload completion probabilities analytically, we define a theoretical bound under stated hypotheses:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Independent Loss Model}: Channel frame loss is modeled as an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Bernoulli error process with packet error rate $p \in [0, 1)$. While the i.i.d. Bernoulli model provides a tractable analytical baseline, physical \ac{LPWAN} channels typically exhibit temporally correlated fading; the stochastic ns-3 simulations evaluated in this work capture these fading dynamics directly without relying on the i.i.d. simplification.
  \item \textit{Bounded Retransmissions}: Selective \ac{SACK} retransmission retries are bounded by the protocol's maximum retry limit $R_{\mathrm{max}} = 5$ (matching \texttt{LoRaMultiPacketConfig::MAX\_RETRIES}).
\end{enumerate}
Under these stated assumptions, the analytical upper bound for successfully reassembling an $N$-fragment payload across $R_{\mathrm{max}}$ retries is given by:
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{complete}}(N, p, R_{\mathrm{max}}) = \left( 1 - p^{R_{\mathrm{max}} + 1} \right)^N
\end{equation}
It is crucial to clarify that when reliable modes (Mode 3 and Mode 4) sustain a 100\% Application Payload Delivery Ratio ($PDR_{\mathrm{app}}$) in Fig.~\ref{fig:ns3_pdr}, this does not indicate physical channel reliability or zero-loss propagation. Rather, it demonstrates that the selective bit-vector \ac{SACK}-\ac{ARQ} mechanism successfully recovers lost fragments within the $R_{\mathrm{max}} = 5$ retry budget prior to session timeout.

In \ac{LoS} conditions, SF10 sustains complete payload reassembly up to 19.1~km, exhibiting a steep drop-off between 20.0~km and 23.1~km as frame loss probability exceeds retransmission recovery budgets. Conversely, best-effort unreliable modes (Mode 1 and Mode 2) experience progressive packet drop rates as spatial fading degrades signal-to-noise ratios.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ns3_goodput} depicts effective application-layer Goodput (bps). While best-effort streaming (Mode 2) achieves peak throughput in pristine channels by omitting acknowledgment latency, Mode 4 (\textit{Reliable Stream}) preserves sustained throughput near physical channel capacity across the operational range, degrading only near receiver sensitivity thresholds.

\subsection{Planned Real-World Field Benchmarking Campaign}
\label{sec:field_benchmarking}
While hardware testbed experiments on two Heltec Wi-Fi \ac{LoRa}~32~V3 nodes have validated the functional correctness, C++ memory safety, \ac{3WHS} state machine transitions, and out-of-order reassembly of LAMP, a systematic outdoor field campaign is planned as future experimental work to record real-world empirical performance under environmental interference, multipath reflections, and temperature-induced frequency drift.

\subsubsection{Planned Experimental Matrix and Evaluation Metrics}
The planned outdoor physical validation strategy follows established \ac{LPWAN} characterization methodologies~\cite{cattani2017}. It will sweep transmission distances (1 to 5000~meters across \ac{LoS} and \ac{NLoS} conditions) and payload sequence sizes (1 to 100 chunks, $\approx 24.6$~KB) under standard radio configurations (SF7, BW 500~kHz, CR 4/5, 10~dBm TX power).

During the planned outdoor campaign, four core performance metrics will be recorded across 5 independent trials per operational mode:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Application Payload Delivery Ratio ($PDR_{\mathrm{app}}$)}: The percentage of application-level payloads successfully reconstructed at the receiver.
  \item \textbf{End-to-End Latency}: Total elapsed duration from initial fragment serialization at the transmitter to final reassembly callback at the receiver.
  \item \textbf{Effective Throughput (Goodput)}: The delivery rate of application payload data, calculated as $\mathrm{PayloadSize\ (bits)} / \mathrm{Latency\ (seconds)}$.
  \item \textbf{Retransmission Overhead}: Count of extra fragments and \ac{SACK} frames transmitted in reliable modes compared to unreliable baselines.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Architectural Roadmap}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presents the design, implementation, network simulation, and hardware validation of LAMP, a lightweight and reliable transport-layer architecture optimized for transmitting segmented large payloads over point-to-point and local multi-node \ac{LoRa} networks. By replacing traditional Stop-and-Wait per-packet acknowledgments with selective retransmissions requested via cumulative binary bitmaps, and eliminating redundant SOM/EOM markers in favor of implicit index derivation, the protocol achieves high reliability under lossy conditions with minimal airtime overhead and duty-cycle footprint.

\subsection{Architectural Roadmap}
To guide the long-term progression of LAMP from point-to-point data links to decentralized multiagent mesh networks, we define a strategic three-level architectural roadmap:

\begin{itemize}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Level 1 -- Addressed Transport \& Polite Spectrum Access (Current Implemented Stack)}: 
  The core architecture established in this work. It features 8-bit source and destination node addressing (\texttt{srcAddr}, \texttt{dstAddr}) with broadcast support (\texttt{0xFF}), dynamic 9-byte header packing, four operational modes (unreliable best-effort datagrams to stateful \ac{SACK}-\ac{ARQ} streams), a \ac{3WHS} for parameter negotiation, and an opt-in \ac{HAL} helper implementing Channel Activity Detection (CAD)/CSMA-CA with exponential backoff for \textit{Polite Spectrum Access} as specified by ETSI~EN~300~220-1~\cite{etsi300220}.
  
  \item \textbf{Level 2 -- Adaptive Spectrum \& Link Optimization (Near-Term Enhancement)}: 
  Extending the link layer to support dynamic channel adaptation in multi-node clusters sharing the same RF channel:
  \begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textit{Adaptive Data Rate (\ac{ADR})}: Dynamically tuning Spreading Factor (SF7--SF12) and Coding Rate (CR) based on real-time RSSI/SNR link quality metrics embedded in \ac{SACK} feedback frames.
    \item \textit{Scheduled Time-Slotted Access}: Incorporating Time Division Multiplexing (TDM) and microsecond time synchronization for collision-free multiagent payload transfers in high-density deployments~\cite{wei2020tdm}.
    \item \textit{Optional Security Profile (Future Work Extension)}: For raw-LoRa multi-node and multi-agent links requiring data privacy, LAMP can be extended with an opt-in Security Profile providing AES-128-GCM or HMAC-SHA256 authentication, payload encryption, and 32-bit sequence replay protection at the transport header level, evaluating byte, latency, and energy overhead in multi-node configurations.
  \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{Level 3 -- Multi-Hop Mesh Extension (Long-Term Vision)}: 
  Scaling the architecture to autonomous multi-hop networks, such as \ac{UAV} swarms, distributed sensor meshes, and relay networks~\cite{kim2022multihop, wong2024mesh}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textit{Mesh Routing Protocol Integration}: Combining dynamic routing (e.g., AODV-Light or RPL adaptation) with hop-by-hop vs. end-to-end \ac{SACK} reliability options to limit retransmission overhead over multi-hop links~\cite{wong2024mesh}.
    \item \textit{IPv6 Network Layer Bridging}: Leveraging the 8-bit Node ID mapping to establish full IPv6 link-local capability (\texttt{fe80::/64}), connecting edge nodes directly to global IP infrastructure.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
